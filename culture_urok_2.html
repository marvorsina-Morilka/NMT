Ephemeral Art — Here today, gone tomorrow
What does ‘ephemeral’ mean? It is used to describe something that only lasts for a short time — like a mayfly, or a pleasure that passes. It is also a genre of art: how can you study art that is here today and gone tomorrow?
Ephemeral art is not about pictures kept in a gallery. It came to prominence in the 1950s. One interpretation is that it promotes awareness of how quickly life changes. Tibetan monks create sand mandalas that are blown away in days.
Some artists use materials that self-destruct. Andy Goldsworthy made a ribbon of poppy petals held by saliva. In 2014, Néle Azevedo made 5,000 tiny ice figures that melted — a memorial that later became a symbol of climate change.
Dutch artist Smilde created a perfect cloud indoors that lasted seconds — spectators experienced humidity and smoke, not just a photo.
Street art, 3D pavement art and sand sculpture are other forms. Graffiti is ephemeral because it is unprotected. In modern society ephemeral art can provoke reactions, raise awareness or simply give pleasure.
